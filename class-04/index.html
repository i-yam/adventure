1How the portfolio is graded

The course is a portfolio examination. The portfolio is the three decks you pitch at the three shark tanks. The first two count a quarter each, the final one half, because by the end the feedback is sharper and we try to get more sharks into the room.

Teams grade as teams. If the work in your team was uneven on a given deliverable (someone was ill and a colleague covered, someone carried the financial model), send me one email with everyone on the team in copy saying who did more and who did less. As long as the team agrees, that is fine and I adjust. If the team does not agree, we have a call with everybody and I mediate. It has happened once. A student claimed he had written the business plan, and within the call it was clear that he did not understand the document, and the document was not far from what a single prompt to a current model produces. I assume good faith. If it is not there, there is a way to deal with it.

The criteria stack up across the blocks:

Shark tankWeightWhat the deck is judged on
1 · Pre-seed (today)25%Is the problem validated and real? Is the size and growth of the opportunity estimated sensibly? Do you present yourselves as the people who can do this? Is the idea clear enough that a customer, an investor or a first hire could follow it?
2 · Seed25%All of the above, plus product/market fit and whether this works as a business: do the numbers make sense, could it pay for itself?
3 · Series A50%All of the above, plus scalability. At this stage nobody can prove scalability, so we invite more sharks and ask them directly whether they believe it scales. A bakery is a business; scaling bakeries takes a franchise model and a go-to-market edge.

Organisationally: Block 2 runs in class as planned. Students with a timetable conflict in December can take Block 3 asynchronously; the lectures will be recorded and posted on YouTube, and these notes cover the same ground. The final pitch event in January is the one to attend in person.

2Failing upwards

One team said before the session that their idea was dying and they would need a new one for the next pitch. Good. That is a learning outcome, not a setback.

I do not advocate working more. I advocate working faster, and the second time you do anything you make fewer mistakes than the first. The ideal trajectory through this course is to fail the first two shark tanks and nail the third. The point of the course is to make as many early-stage founder mistakes as possible before you put your own money and years on the line.

The sharks' verdicts do not enter your grade. The grade is based on what your deck shows: a validated problem, a sensibly estimated opportunity, a team that can do it. If every shark says "I still don't buy it", that is an important signal for you, and it happens in life, but it does not lower your mark. The tragic truth is that you can be right and still lose. A large part of founder chutzpah is being able to lose upwards.

If you are afraid of pitching, go to an open-mic night at a stand-up club and do five minutes. Most jokes told to a room of tipsy strangers do not land, even prepared ones you find funny, and the experience of a joke dying in front of people is enormously useful. If you can survive that, you can pitch a business to anyone; almost every other room is less stressful.

Last point: the portfolio can work for your CV. A strong concept, a landing page or a deck you are proud of is something to show if your next step is a startup job or a venture fund.

3The sharks

Three sharks joined remotely: we cannot fly people from London to Würzburg for an afternoon yet. The format was ten minutes of pitch, then questions, then each shark says yes or no to one question: does it make sense for this team to keep investing their time and energy in this idea, or would they be better off killing it?

  • Abdullah Abdelrazek, co-founder of KLAO. Took this course two years ago, met his co-founder in it, and now runs a Würzburg startup doing automated translation for government bodies and NGOs. As alumni tend to be, a harsh shark.
  • Pavel Goldman-Kalyadin, co-founder of Surprisal Labs, a Berlin cybersecurity startup that discovers threats by exploration rather than by known signatures; previously on the machine learning team at Sumsub. Closed his pre-seed round two weeks before the session.
  • Alexey Shagraev, founder of Lovi, ex-Google. Recently closed a large deal without giving up equity, and has sat on something like ten of these panels. The most dedicated shark we have, and the one most willing to say no.

The pitches in the order they ran follow.

4The grandchild trick, priced

The scam-protection team opened again with the founder's grandmother, who lives alone and keeps getting calls from strangers claiming her son has had an accident and needs money. Then the figures, from German police statistics on the team's slides: around 9,400 reported cases and close to €98 million in damage, which is roughly €10,000 per case; fake police officers up 64 percent from 2024 to 2025; the grandchild trick about as large. The best moment was from their interviews. The grandmother's first answer was that the idea was wonderful and she was proud of him, which the team correctly called a compliment, not evidence. Her second answer was the useful one: she does not want to be monitored and does not want to lose her independence. The team had not thought of that, and it reshaped the solution: a short self-paced course that turns panic into a pause, optional practice with a simulated scam call, and a cheat sheet for the fridge (stay calm, hang up, call back on a number you know, hand over nothing). Why now: AI clones voices for the fraudsters and detects fakes for the defenders. The ask: contacts in crime prevention and legal advice. The team went to ten minutes, which was the limit.

The sharks:

  • How does this make money? Pavel's first question, and Abdullah's verdict: courses are hard to scale into a business, and if the AI-detection part is real, European rules on call processing will be a serious obstacle (he could not use Apple's live call translation in Germany without switching his phone's region). No.
  • Reframe it. Alexey said yes, with two conditions. First, courses are the least sexy thing in the world and no VC invests in them, so pitch the courses as the deliberately unscalable start: it is how you meet real customers, collect data and learn the distribution, and the scalable product comes out of that. Do unscalable things, but know that you are doing them. Second, research monetisation properly. Charging people to learn how not to be robbed is a hard sell, and nobody pays for a risk that has not happened yet. Connect it to big money instead: insurers who lower premiums if you complete the training or install the tool, or corporate security programmes. Yes.
  • Sell to the pipe. Pavel: the grandmother is not the customer; her phone company and her bank are. Telcos and banks lose when their customers lose money and would rather partner with a promising startup than build this themselves. Supply the technology to them. Ideally one of them buys you in a year. No, specifically to training courses.

Read the three answers together and they agree. The problem is real, nobody doubted it. The customer is wrong. Find the party that already loses money when the scam succeeds, and sell to them.

5Detection, not exercises

The workplace mental-health team opened with a Monday that starts with 47 emails, three deadlines and a conflict in the team, and introduced Lisa again: 34, leads a team, learning by burning. Their figures: mental-health conditions account for a large share of sick days in Germany, with absences averaging 34 days. This time there was a live demo, which is exactly what the room asked for last week. A learning library across six skills (emotional regulation, stress, conflict, boundaries and saying no, time management, recovery). An AI check-in inside Teams that reads signals such as 5.5 hours of meetings, 62 app switches an hour and 20 minutes of focused work, asks how Lisa is doing, suggests a lesson, reshuffles her calendar and blocks focus time. A team dashboard for managers. Why now: AI makes coaching cheap enough for every employee, mental health is less taboo, and German employers must assess psychological strain and can spend up to €600 per employee a year tax-free on prevention.

  • One more tool. Alexey has seen many of these and has not seen one work. When you are drowning, the last thing you need is one more tool asking how you are and offering a three-minute exercise. The demo also did not demonstrate the claim: it could have been scripted dialogue, with no visible AI and no visible psychology. And choose one story: either you make people healthier, or you found a way to make money from a regulation. "We help people, and luckily the law makes companies buy us" does not hold together. No.
  • Invest in trust. Pavel agreed that corporate wellbeing chatbots are everywhere and nobody trusts them; his previous employer had several and nobody used them. If you can show that you put most of your effort into doing it right, trust becomes the positioning in a crowded market. A small no.
  • Move the AI to detection. Abdullah said yes on one condition: do not be a chatbot. Live inside the company's ecosystem, its chat, meeting and ticket systems, and detect the patterns of overload from there, with the follow-up done by people, or by partner organisations in psychology. In one line: AI for detection, people for prevention. Yes.

That is the same conclusion as last week's notes, reached independently by a shark: the valuable, hard part is spotting people at risk early. Pavel added a note on scale: investors in this space expect the problem stated ten times bigger. Not "we help stressed team leads", but "everyone on the planet mentally healthy". Think big enough that nobody can tell you you are not bold.

6What happens between dives

The diving team positioned the app as Strava for a sport Strava ignores. The problem, sharper than last week: information is scattered across apps, finding a good site and a buddy is hard, and your diving history lives in three places (certification in one app, logbook in another). Their figures: around 9 million active scuba divers, 30 million certifications, an estimated 139,000 dives a day. One app for a community feed, live dive maps, a digital logbook with AI analysis, and a digital certification card. The pricing slide was the most thoughtful in the session: most people dive two or three times a year on holiday and hate paying for unused subscriptions, so there is a free tier, a day pass at $1, a week at $3 and a month at $5, less than a tenth of the cost of one dive. Better delivery than last week, too.

  • Niche social networks are a thing. Pavel: this will not be the next Facebook, but niche networks work, nothing exists for divers, and the market is decent. Look at komoot from Potsdam, which for years sold maps one-off by region: you paid for the area you were about to explore, which fits a sport done on holiday. It later moved to a subscription and was bought by Bending Spoons in 2025. His worry was frequency: you run every week and dive twice a year, so users forget the app for months. Find a reason to open it between dives, as hikers plan summer routes in winter. Yes.
  • More revenue lines. Abdullah did not think subscriptions alone bring enough money. Add commissions from dive centres and other partners. The team's own answer pointed the same way: dive schools need a place to show their offers, and in the long run the app could issue its own certification, which costs divers €350 to €700. Yes.
  • Where is the competition? Alexey asked about Oceanic+ and Garmin Dive. The team had downloaded Oceanic+ and read Reddit complaints, but the competitors were not in the deck at all, which is a red flag in itself. And "they have no community" is a finding, not an answer: why do divers not talk there? A product problem, or do they talk on Instagram? Each answer changes your idea. Everyone wants a community; communities are very hard to build. Show that you understand why the incumbents do not have one and why you will. He kept his verdict for next time.

7The parenting license

The parenting team narrowed their audience since last week: well-educated, financially stable, highly motivated families, the group that social work and state programmes do not serve. The founder who is a social worker spoke from experience: there are many interventions for families in crisis and none for "us". The evidence slide cited a review of 21 studies showing that evidence-based parenting programmes improve children's behaviour and reduce parental stress, plus the WHO's recommendation of such programmes. Then the provocation: a parenting license, voluntary, more onboarding than permit, delivered first as a website with daily learning nuggets, then e-learning modules, then an academy with personal coaching and community. The ask came down from €250,000 to €50,000 for 10 percent.

  • Make it the birth-rate story. Pavel, father of a two-year-old, said yes. In Germany the medical side is well covered (midwives visit new parents at home), the practical and bureaucratic side is not. The bigger frame: every politician says Europe needs more children, especially from well-off families, and a startup that says "we help people decide to have kids and make it easier" could get a lot of attention. He had just been in Singapore, where the low birth rate is the government's top concern. And partner with insurers: statutory insurance already pays for birth-preparation courses. Yes.
  • You do not need the €50,000. Both Pavel and Abdullah: you can build the pilot yourselves for close to nothing, and should not give away equity for it. Abdullah asked what you will actually offer in the first three months. Answer: a website and social media to raise interest, free e-learning samples, then paid modules ending in the license. Yes.
  • What is it? Alexey: a parenting license sounds like the most European thing he has heard in years, and not in a good way. More importantly, he did not understand what the product does day to day. "Parenting is hard so we help" is an intention, not a product. You do not have to show every module, but the room has to understand what kind of help, why it matters, and what parents say about it. On the birth-rate frame he disagreed: governments care about many things that have no money in them, and media coverage does not drive revenue on its own. Where is the money? No explicit verdict, but read it as a no.

This was the pitch the sharks argued about among themselves, which is rare and worth noticing: the topic has energy. Energy is not revenue, but it is something to build on. The naming worked as a provocation: it made everyone react, and the team knew it.

8Between eight and four

The tradespeople team opened with a show of hands: who has waited all day at home for a plumber? Keep your hand up if you were told anything more precise than "between eight and four". Trades still work the way doctors did ten years ago: customers block a day, small firms with 3 to 30 technicians cannot plan, one office person answers calls, writes quotes and plans routes at once, and the technician often does not know what is broken until he is on site and missing the spare part. The product: a booking page that reads the firm's calendar, adds real travel time between jobs with a buffer, and uses AI to classify the request (from text or a photo) into a duration and a parts list, so the offered slots are realistic, not just free. The firm gets a back office to accept or reject requests; an AI phone assistant can route callers to it. Price: €49 to €149 a month by number of technicians, cheaper than field-service software at €60 to €300 and AI phone assistants at €85 to €99. Market: about 97,000 heating, plumbing and electrical firms in Germany. The honest note was the best part: they had talked to a moving company, which had fewer of the expected problems and different ones.

  • Yes, dig deeper. Alexey: in the UK this exists sometimes, and the real problem is that firms cannot plan because they do not know the jobs in advance; and good luck predicting London traffic, so estimates are either very pessimistic or always late. Go and find out from the tradesperson's side why it really happens. He liked that the team was clear about the limits of its current stage. Yes.
  • Everyone got used to it. Pavel: people wait once or twice a year, tell their manager they are working from home, and accept it. If both sides find the status quo acceptable, a disruptor gets pushback, not gratitude. The question is not the technology, it is how you change a habit. No.
  • What is your USP? Abdullah asked, and the answer was travel time plus job recognition. Scheduling tools exist; those two features do not, as far as the team knows. His concern: neither is hard to copy, and a competitor with resources builds them next quarter. Find something defensible. A hesitant yes.

9Size it like a company

The warehouse team, all from the AI master's with industry backgrounds including years as SAP warehouse consultants, pitched an AI optimisation layer for slotting, deciding where each product sits so that picking is fast. Today slotting is a one-off consulting project, redone every few years, and at least two warehouses in the region use rule-based distance metrics. Their figures: data-driven slotting can cut picking walking distance by about 24 percent; in a 2026 survey of 400 warehouse professionals, 61 percent called labour management extremely challenging and 81 percent were interested in AI, with cost and integration the barriers. The architecture: reinforcement learning on historical and simulated data, an explainability layer (LIME, layer-wise relevance propagation, reward analysis), and a language model that explains each recommendation to the warehouse manager, who approves the critical ones. It plugs into SAP EWM via OData. Plan: 25 warehouses in three years at €25,000 a year each. Ask: warehouse managers for pilots.

  • Why so careful? Alexey: a cool presentation and a cool approach, and deeply unambitious. Small steps, human checks, European regulation, in a world where Amazon warehouses run on robots. If you take the venture road, build something big. He could not say yes. When the team answered that robots still need good slotting and that their ambition is no German warehouse without AI, including industrial and construction warehouses, he said: that is already much better, now take five more steps down that road. And why only Germany? No.
  • Abdullah was not convinced by the integration path or the market. No.

Two notes I gave the team. First, the sales plan is far too slow. Twenty-five customers in three years is one a quarter. Three founders, one of them selling half-time, and you expect a single deal a year? A realistic early curve looks more like zero in year one, ten in year two, a hundred in year three. Long sales cycles are an argument for starting the conversations now, not for a flat plan. Second, the session had no demo. You present impressive experience and a clever architecture and the audience waits for the moment where the messy warehouse becomes the tidy one, and nothing happens. You can build a simple digital warehouse simulator yourselves, and THWS has a physical logistics lab in Schweinfurt with people who would talk to you. If it works and plays well with SAP, it might even be a natural acquisition for SAP. I said yes, reluctantly, so that the team keeps going and fixes the numbers.

11What the sharks kept saying

Six pitches, three people, and the same handful of notes came back again and again.

  • Follow the big money. The person who has the problem is often not the one who pays. Scam protection: banks, telcos, insurers. Parenting: insurers. Mental health: employers, but only with a coherent story. Ask who already loses money when the problem happens.
  • Unscalable starts are fine if you know they are unscalable. Courses, home visits, hand-curated content: good ways to meet customers and learn. Say so in the pitch, and say what the scalable product is that comes out of it.
  • Be ambitious. Alexey's closing words: the whole venture business is looking for wildly ambitious projects, knowing most will fail, because the ones that succeed change the world. At this stage investors look at the team and the vision. If you play small, it is a lifestyle business, which is a perfectly fine thing to build, just not with venture money. Show the transformative version of your idea, even though you are years away from it.
  • Know your competitors better than they do. Not just names: why they lack what you are building.
  • Be specific. Vision and good intentions are not enough; the room has to understand what the product does.
  • Talk to customers who pay. Abdullah's closing advice, and a lesson his own company is learning right now: before anything else, go to potential customers and make sure someone actually wants to pay for your solution. Do not rely on your own thinking or on friends saying it is a good idea. It is the fastest way not to spend two years on something that does not work.

12What the room said afterwards

After the break we went round the room: what was hard, what was easy, what you will do differently.

  • The sharks were online. One team had not expected that and spent energy adjusting the camera. Next time, plan your setup for a remote audience: one laptop on the call, the room on the big screen.
  • The clock. I said five minutes at the start when I meant ten, which created useless extra pressure. My mistake. The team that heard five and finished early did fine.
  • Money. Several teams said the hardest feedback was about how they make money. That is what the next three weeks are for: the financial model and product/market fit, so that in December you present a business model with numbers. You do not have to have all the numbers now. But if you show numbers, you must be able to defend them. Sometimes no numbers are better than numbers that are clearly off.
  • The USP. One team found it very hard to state the USP crystal clear when a shark asked for it again. Ideally your elevator pitch already contains it, because people get distracted, and ten minutes is a long time.

My note on delivery. There was a lot of air in the pitches: moments of hanging, of repeating yourselves, no dynamic. Next time you will have about the same time and be expected to put much more into it. And energy: several of you started on a high note and deflated like a balloon within a minute. As a founder you need full energy for eight hours a day, with employees, clients and investors. Ten minutes should be possible. The one founder who was visibly excited about his product from the first second to the last was the clearest pitch of the day, whatever the numbers.

Not everyone has to be a salesperson. Channel who you are. The social worker on the parenting team was very relatable because she wove her own coaching experience into the pitch, and paired it with a deliberately provocative name. That combination was on purpose, and it worked. Think about how you present, not only what you present. Doing both at once is hard. Try anyway.

13Nerves are fuel

Several of you were clearly nervous. Good. Remember the feeling. Many professional actors say that if you are not nervous before a show, you should not be an actor. Anyone who does extreme sports knows adrenaline is a resource: it sharpens your senses and focuses you, if you learn to use it. Standing in front of a group of people and inviting them to judge you goes against every safety signal a social animal has. Of course you are nervous.

Keep perspective, though. Today the stakes were a course. When the stakes are your company, potentially worth millions, today's nervousness will be the lowest you ever feel before a pitch. Learn now how to channel it so that it does not work against you.

14Look for unfiltered feedback

The sharks were relatable, and they were honest. They did not filter. In my experience, people who have tried to build something value honesty above almost everything when they talk to other builders. They have limited time, no stake in your project, and they want you to make the best of the time they give you.

In education, feedback is too often not honest. You are adults and you feel that too. One reason this course is built around shark tanks is that I want you to experience feedback that is unfiltered and constructive: maybe a bit hurtful, never offensive.

This applies far beyond startups. When you land your first job, pay attention to whether you get feedback in this form. Honest feedback is how you grow fastest and how you avoid getting stuck early in your career. If the place you land filters everything into politeness, consider whether it is the right place. The filter is noise, and it makes it much harder to understand how to grow, not as a founder, just as a professional.
