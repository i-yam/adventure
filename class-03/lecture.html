Six teams pitched today, with no time limit, to a room of their classmates. Next week the same teams pitch in ten minutes to people who are not their classmates and have no reason to be kind. These notes are what the room said in between.

The session ran the way the course runs: pitch first, get hit, then hear the theory. One team asked for the theory first. The answer is that if you already knew how to pitch, I would have nothing to teach you, and the fastest way to find out what you do not know is to stand up. The four parts of Class 03 (the six boxes, the story, be kind to the room, red-team the problem) carry the exercises; this page carries the pitches, the feedback and the argument behind the parts. Section 9 is the summary to reread before the shark tank.

1The fridge and the warehouse

The first team opened with a question to the room: how do you organise your fridge? Yoghurt in front, things you rarely use at the back, and when you wake up late and need to grab breakfast, the organised fridge is the one that does not stress you. Then the turn: scale that up and you are in warehouse management, with four hundred thousand articles instead of twenty. Put the most-used item on the top shelf and every pick costs time, time costs stress, and the forklifts collide. By the team's survey figures, 80 to 90 percent of warehouse management still runs on Excel, and most of the four hundred managers asked wanted AI as the next step. The twist: a manager with ten years of experience will not trust a black box that reshuffles the shelves, so whatever optimises the layout also has to explain itself. The solution: an AI that goes beyond Manhattan distance, saves time, and is explainable enough to convince the people who pay for it.

The opening worked. The room was with them, answering questions, laughing. Then the pitch asked whether anyone watches videos of people organising fridges, nobody did, and the room was dropped. If you drop people, you have to pick them up again, and that is harder than not dropping them. It is a messaging note, not a content note, but it is the note the room gave first.

The content notes:

  • Jargon. Nobody in the room knew what Manhattan distance is. (In Manhattan the streets are a grid, so you cannot walk a straight line from A to B; you walk along the blocks, and the distance is longer. A warehouse is a grid of shelves. That is all.) If you use a term that implies expert knowledge, either make it self-explanatory or be sure you are in a room that already knows it. Warehouse managers do. Investors in AI companies may not.
  • The metaphor. The fridge got people in and then would not let them out. A good metaphor carries you; a metaphor you cannot leave becomes the pitch. The warehouse problem, the trade-off between a layout that is optimal and a layout that humans can understand, is different from the fridge problem, and the room lost it on the way back from the fridge. Asked honestly whether the point came across, the answer was no.
  • The ask. Nobody could say what the team wanted from the room. The answer, once asked, was a pilot with an industrial partner. Say that.
  • How. The solution was "AI", and then things work. The team's defence was fair: the real answer is neurosymbolic AI, explaining it takes longer than five minutes, and the room is business students. That is exactly the problem the next section is about.

2The rug salesman

One of the people in The Venture Mindset is Pejman Nozad, who arrived in California from Iran, worked in a car wash, and then got a job selling Persian rugs at a gallery on University Avenue in Palo Alto. People who buy expensive rugs are people with money, and in Palo Alto people with money are investors. He talked to his customers, remembered them, and at some point technical founders worked out that if you pitched Nozad well, he could carry the pitch to people you could not reach. He is now one of the most prominent seed investors in the Valley, with Dropbox and DoorDash among the early bets, and ranked first on the Forbes Midas seed list two years running (Hustle Fund, Slush).

The lesson for the warehouse team is not about rugs. The background of the person you pitch to should not matter. What matters is that the point comes across in a form that person can capture, remember and carry to the next person. Because that is how your pitch actually travels:

  • An engineer in the client company understands you at a technical level and vouches for you: these people know their stuff. The engineer does not sign.
  • The engineer introduces you to a decision maker, who needs an intuition for why they are paying. Not the mechanism. The intuition.
  • The decision maker has a budget, say twenty thousand euros a year for a software subscription, which a single site manager may be able to approve. They still have to explain upward what they bought.

At every level somebody needs a short, intuitive account of what the thing does. It has to carry some complexity, because if it is dumb and simple, why pay twenty thousand for it. It also has to be explainable, because if nobody can say what it does, why pay twenty thousand for it. Managing that trade-off between complexity and intuition is a skill, and the only way to acquire it is to pitch a lot. That is why this course makes you pitch a lot.

3Parenthood without onboarding

Two founders, a social worker who coaches parents and children and a logistics-and-marketing person, promised emotions, numbers and an idea. The emotions: you become a parent with no preparation, and every parent says the same sentences afterwards. If I had known. Nobody told me. I thought it would be easier. I feel left alone. The numbers, from the team's slides: 62 percent of mothers report being strongly burdened, 36 percent of families report conflict, money gets tight, and Germany has about 650,000 births a year, so twice that many new parents, every year, as a recurring moment to reach them. The idea: a parenting academy, e-learning, coaching and community in an app, twelve modules, voluntary, no test, no gatekeeping. The ask: €250,000 to build the academy and ship the app, with the app first because it scales, and face-to-face coaching in two or three years.

The problem landed. One student said the moment the pain points went up, the whole room was in. The pictures were good. The founders opened with their faces and closed with their faces, which is a good habit: people who hear ten pitches a day forget whom they scheduled the call with. What was missing from the team slide was why these two are the right people, which they are, but the pitch did not connect their backgrounds to the product.

Then the money. The room's first reaction was that the ask was high for what exists, which is nothing yet. It could be a legitimate ask with traction. For a product like this, traction is cheap: vibe-code a website with content, curate the best YouTube material on the topic into usable playlists, put sharing buttons on it, run a fifty-euro ad campaign with precise targeting, and watch whether people stay and whether they tell each other. Future parents and early parents talk to each other a great deal; they build communities around kindergartens. If the demand is there it will show at that scale. Then the ask changes shape: we spent fifty euros and got a hundred visitors, so with a marketing budget of this size we get half a million, and here is what we do with them. That is an ask an investor can check. "Give us €250,000 to build an app" is not.

The second note was about the audience. The slide with three numbers jumped between three audiences: burdened mothers, conflicted families, worried parents. Do they overlap? Maybe the stressed person is a father who is the single earner, the financial worry is his, and it causes the conflict. Then the product is a financial-planning course for expectant fathers on low to medium incomes, the pricing is obvious (cheap), the targeting is obvious, and the ad writes itself: you are about to be a father, it will be more stressful than you think, a fifth of couples who cannot handle money pressure separate in the first three years, here are clear short lessons so your family does not fall apart. The conversion on that is going to be much higher than on "future parents, we have content about everything". Or the audience is single mothers, smaller, but under far more pressure. Or gay couples about to have children, for whom there is almost no content and who may pay more. Each of these is a different product, a different price, a different channel.

The principle: at the early stage, you want the people in the most pain, for whom you deliver the most value per euro. Start with one audience you can reach, understand and validate, and broaden once you have them. Facebook was a network for one campus, then two. Nobody starts general.

4The grandchild trick

The third team started from a phone call. One founder's grandmother had been rung by someone claiming to be her son, in trouble, needing money. She was shaken, called her grandson, and he was at work. The people who fall for this feel ashamed and tell nobody. The team's figures from German police statistics put it at roughly ten thousand reported cases a year and close to a hundred million euros in damage, about ten thousand euros per case, with fake police officers and the grandchild trick as the two biggest categories. The motivation slide was personal: our parents are getting older, we will too, this touches every family. The why-now slide was the best in the session: AI cuts both ways, it gives fraudsters synthetic voices and gives defenders a way to recognise a scam in real time. The goal: less fear, more safety, more independence. The solution, spoken rather than shown: a training programme for older people, delivered in person, so that the next call does not catch them cold.

The room's notes:

  • No solution slide. The problem and the why-now built real tension, and the solution arrived in spoken words only. It is the thing you are selling. Put it on a slide.
  • Personal motivation, up to a point. It works, and it is cheap to overdo. Elizabeth Holmes pitched Theranos from her childhood fear of needles, and the documentary about her is in cinemas now (see the Class 01 notes). You also have to deliver what you promise.
  • The name. The working title paired "time to think" with elderly people, and several in the room found it uncomfortable, closer to dementia than to safety. A product name has to work in the sentence your customer will say to someone else: "I bought X for my mother." Test it in that sentence.
  • Where is the return? A student asked why anyone would invest: a good cause, but how does the money come back? It is a fair question and, at this stage, a rarely asked one. When a team has an idea and no traction, investors ask how people solve the problem today, what they pay for that, whether you have talked to customers, and where their price sensitivity sits. Both sides know the final product will differ from the slide. What convinces at the very beginning is that the problem is real and you understand it; one angel who believes in the team or the problem is usually how the first money arrives. The business model becomes the salient question once there is traction. A screenshot that went round X last week: a startup emailing a customer to ask whether it may cancel his paid subscription and refund him, because before he subscribed investors asked about the addressable market, and now they ask why revenue is forty-nine dollars a month.

On why-now in general: most of the problems you pitch are old. Parenting is arguably better than it has ever been; phone fraud is decades old. So the question "why has nobody done this" will come up, and you need the answer: two years ago this was not possible, in three years someone else will own it, now there is this new lever. Vine had short video before TikTok and died, because mobile internet had not penetrated far enough to give it a market. MySpace had the social network before Facebook and lost, not on features (Facebook ran on PHP for a large part of its history) but on how it recruited users and scaled. Timing kills good companies in both directions.

5Permission to rest

The fourth team opened with a sketch: a colleague buried in meetings, deciding not to come in tomorrow. Then the numbers from their slides: a large share of sick days in Germany is attributed to mental-health conditions, most of those to depression, absences are long, and the count rises year on year. Meet Lisa, 34, team lead of eight at an 800-person company, three deadlines, a conflict in her team, 47 unread emails, says yes to everything, never trained to handle stress or conflict. Learning by burning. The causes they found: heavy workload, constant digital communication, unclear priorities, no formal training in conflict or boundaries, and home office, where the stress signals are noticed later. The solution: three-minute personalised micro-exercises on six skills (stress, emotions, conflict, boundaries, priorities, recovery), inside Teams or Slack, privacy-first, sold B2B per employee per month, starting with a paid pilot. The why-now was strong: AI makes coaching cheap enough for every employee, hybrid work has put everyone in Teams, and German employers are obliged to assess psychological strain at work and can spend up to €600 per employee per year tax-free on prevention under § 3 Nr. 34 EStG.

The room's notes. "Problem" followed immediately by "big market" sounds wrong when the problem is depression; find a different joint. The overall impression, said one student, was that the cure for overwork was more screen time. The team's answer: the lessons are done in work time and make the rest of the work easier. Another student liked the roles slide, one expert per field, and that is the slide the first teams were missing. Several in the room started designing the product: push a notification at lunch so people actually take the break, link it to the calendar, tell them to look out of the window for thirty seconds, go for a walk. One said the external nudge is the point: you do not take the break unless something outside you makes it legitimate.

That is the real product. People burn out partly because they have no permission structure for rest, culturally or psychologically. A tool that gives them one, and justifies it to the business as fewer sick days, has a foot in the door: use this and burnout-related absence drops from thirty-odd days a year to twenty. Delivering on that is hard, and it is where the AI belongs: detecting people at risk early, because by the time someone is posting gibberish in Slack and logging off you have lost them. I know an engineer who shipped a major open-source system more or less alone in a year, pushed the last commit and did not come back for a year. Early detection is the hard, valuable part. The second part is getting people off the screen and towards other people: in an office, find the colleagues who handle stress well, train them a little, and pair the fragile ones with them for a random coffee or a lunch. In a home office it is much harder, and I do not know the answer.

One more thing. Notice how the room discussed this pitch compared with the others: everyone had an opinion on how to build it. That is the signal you look for in customer development. When potential customers keep telling you how to build your product, you have hit the audience. When they say "very nice presentation, thank you", you have missed. This team can customer-develop half the room.

6Beneath the surface

The fifth team pitched an app for divers: find a buddy, discover dive spots on a live map, plan trips, log dives from the dive computer, learn for the next certification, a community feed in one place instead of WhatsApp, Facebook and Reddit. By their numbers, six to nine million active divers worldwide and a much larger number ever certified; Germany first, then Switzerland and Austria, then global; dive schools and clubs as the first channel; a core and a pro tier, with AI analysis of your dive data in pro; priced at about ten percent of a dive, around six euros, when a dive costs fifty to seventy. (Check the certification figure before Class 04; PADI alone reports about thirty million certifications in its history, not hundreds of millions.)

My first note was about delivery. Both founders dive, and love it, and none of that reached the room. The pitch about an adventure sport sounded like a quarterly report. Whatever else you fix, fix that.

The content question was: what is the problem? The audience was clear, the feature list was complete, and it was not clear what hurts. The room's attempts to find it were useful. One student runs a surfing group that trains together in Würzburg before the holidays; is there a diving equivalent? A climber pointed at maps: there is no good, validated source of dive sites, especially caves, and a map with difficulty levels, warnings and a gamified progression could be the hook. Another pointed at Flighty, which sells short passes for a trip rather than a year, and asked whether a holiday pass fits a sport most people do on holiday.

My note was retention. Strava works because running is frequent: every month you weigh the subscription against the runs you logged, and the community nudges you to run more. Most divers dive a few times a year. If someone installs your app in February and their next dive is in May, why are they still paying in August? Notifications will not save you: Instagram sends them too, with similar content. If you only keep the people who dive ten times a year, that is a fine target, but then the market is not six to nine million, and the slide should say so. The angle I can see is content: underwater footage is hard to get, beautiful, and most divers already own a GoPro. A social network where divers monetise their footage, with enough curation that the feed looks like the slides did, might hold attention between dives; a slow-television stream of reefs for the office screen might even sell. Community for its own sake is very hard to design. Study the market before you decide.

7Which ad made the money

The last team opened with a show of hands: who has paid for an ad, and who knows which ad produced a sale? Fewer hands the second time. Their example: a fitness studio spends €412 across campaigns and gets 31 bookings, about €13 each, which is fine, except that one campaign spent €121 for a single booking. Small businesses run three or four campaigns across Instagram, TikTok and Google and cannot tell which converts. The product: tracking via pixels and tag manager, a live dashboard per campaign, stop the useless ones, scale the rest, weekly analysis. Then a second product: automated replies on Instagram, WhatsApp and email, with an AI that estimates a quote for a removals company while the owner is busy. Price: €500 to €600 a month, pitched as four times cheaper than an agency, which only sends a PDF at the end of the month.

The room's notes. One student who described herself as a complete beginner at ads, exactly the customer, said she did not understand what was being offered. Someone who runs ads asked how many hours a client takes when tracking breaks, and what the model learns from in month one when Google's own campaigns already learn. Someone asked why this beats Google Performance Max; the honest answer is that Performance Max optimises for reach and for Google's revenue, and the team's product is for people who cannot work Google's tools, do not have an agency, and have to deal with Meta, TikTok and X on top. That is a niche, and a real one: competence and time, not just money.

My note was about the bundle. Two products with different buyers (the owner cares about missed calls; the marketing person, if there is one, cares about campaigns), different pricing, different risk (who pays when the AI quotes a removal wrong and the margin was thin), and no option to buy one without the other. Estimating what to charge for a job is the hardest thing any freelancer does, and I doubt an AI does it reliably yet. The first product is the clean one. And if you have a website that audits a business's ads, demo it on a business the room can see out of the window. A live demo beats any slide. The internet in the room failed when the team tried, which is also a lesson: have the screenshots.

8Ninety seconds of DoorDash

Y Combinator asks applicants for a video of under two minutes. DoorDash's, from 2013, is embedded in YC's own post on the company, from application to IPO. Watch it before you time your own pitch. The structure, in ninety seconds:

  • Team first, briefly. Four people, Stanford computer science, platform engineering, the founding team of Vevo, product at Square. A flex, but a functional one: when one of them later says "pretty neat routing and batching algorithms" and moves on, the credentials have already answered the question of whether the algorithms are real. An investor can dig on the call. The room does not need to.
  • How they found the problem. "We wanted to build software for small business owners but had no idea what." Then a hundred interviews. The problem comes out of customer development, said plainly: restaurants in Palo Alto want to deliver and cannot afford to; customers want delivery; drivers have idle time. Three sides, each with a reason to say yes. That is the whole explanation of the solution.
  • Traction. They delivered the orders themselves. In the first month, with no real marketing, over 150 paying customers and over $10,000 in sales.

It does not feel rushed. You are with the story, you understand everything, and it is over in a minute and a half. Ten minutes next week is a lot of time.

10Story, ask, and the dream job

The six boxes are the content. The story is the order you say them in, and it starts with the elevator sentence from Class 01: in ten seconds, who you are and what you do, ideally memorable. Part 2 is that exercise.

Then the ask, which was missing or vague in most pitches today and is the subject of Part 3. The room has to know what shelf to put you on. Early on the ask is either money, at this stage a hundred to two hundred thousand at most, with a plausible plan for what it buys, or something that is not money: introductions to twenty warehouse operators, forty dive schools, a franchise owner. The ask can be wrong, you can change it between meetings, but it has to be concrete, because the person you pitched will remember you as "the people from Würzburg who were looking for X" or not at all.

This is the same advice I give about jobs. When someone asks what you want to do after graduating, "a job" is forgotten instantly. "Data analyst for a football club" is remembered, precisely because it is rare and specific, and the next time I am near a football club I may remember there was a student who wanted that. A student in an earlier run of this course answered without hesitating: analyst at a venture capital firm. He interviewed at several, did not like them, and is now at the Bundesbank in Frankfurt. Not VC exactly. But the clear answer is what got him into the rooms.

11Red team and radical candor

Before you pitch, attack your own pitch. Think like the room: what can go wrong, where is the money, what is the weakest number. Part 4 makes you write the three attacks down with the evidence that answers each.

The book for this is Kim Scott's Radical Candor. Scott ran AdSense and other teams at Google and later worked at Apple, and her model has two axes: do you challenge directly, and do you care personally (Stanford GSB). Her example is a presenter whose fly is open. In a culture that neither challenges nor cares, nobody says anything and everyone laughs behind the presenter's back; she calls it manipulative insincerity. In a culture that challenges but does not care, someone shouts "your fly is open" and the room laughs at you while you stand there: obnoxious aggression. In a culture that cares but does not challenge, nobody laughs and nobody tells you: ruinous empathy, which she says is the most common failure. Radical candor is the colleague who asks you to step outside for a second, tells you, and lets you come back and present. Honesty is uncomfortable in the moment and indispensable over time, because without it there is no signal between people, and a business runs on signal.

That is the culture of the shark tank. I have asked the sharks to be honest about what they see. It will look like today, except the room will not be your classmates.

12Say it out loud

Today had no time limit, and every team found out that pitching takes longer than it looks. You always get stuck on the slide you thought was easy, and then you chase the clock. The only cure is to rehearse with the slides and say the words out loud; there is no shortcut. The skill transfers: a person who can pitch a company can present results to a manager and sell a product to a customer, so none of this time is wasted whatever you do next.

Two aids. Students from an earlier run built an AI pitch coach: upload a deck, press start, and several simulated investors (a finance person, a customer champion, and so on) give you verbal feedback structured on the model from this course. I tried it on the Pleias deck and it said, correctly, that the customer problem and pricing were not readable enough. It has bugs; a link follows once it is stable, and you are welcome as early testers. Spoiler: any model you already use does the same job. Tell it what kind of investor it is, give it the deck, give it the recording of your pitch if you have one, paste in this class's pages from the repository as the criteria, and ask it to be honest. It usually is.

On sharing the pitch between team members: good, it shows a team, but today I saw no pitch where I could tell why this person was presenting this part. If you introduce the AI person, the AI person should present the technical part; the psychologist presents why the content works; the business person presents the business. Then, when I have a question later, I can address it to a person rather than a slide number, because I remember who owned what. Watch how Apple brings people on stage: each one is introduced in the context of what they are about to show. You are not Apple yet. The logic still applies.

13Next week: the sharks

Shark Tank #1 is the full session. Ten minutes per team, hard stop, then questions and feedback from the sharks; with six or seven projects that is two hours, and the sharks' time is the scarce resource. Two are confirmed: a THWS graduate who leads business development and internal startups at one of the region's manufacturing groups, and, joining remotely, the founder of a skincare app, ex-Google, who recently raised twenty-five million. A third is pending.

These are real people, so be honest with them. The point of a pitch is to get something that moves you forward. If you need money, ask for money. If you need introductions, customers, a pilot or advice, ask for that. Polish what you have, fix the ask, and rehearse against the clock.
