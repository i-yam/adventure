Ninety-four dollars went into a document that lawyers read line by line before it was filed with the United States government.

It was true. It was also the average across three years, in a business where the cost had been climbing the whole time, which meant it described a company that no longer existed. Nobody was punished for it. Analysts pulled the filing apart within days and published their own figures, and the ones that stuck were between two hundred and fifty and four hundred and sixty dollars.

You are about to do the same thing with your market size, and there is no lawyer in the room.

Market size gets faked more than any other slide because it is the only one where nobody can check you inside a meeting. So size it three ways and show the disagreement.

Top down starts with somebody else's industry figure and cuts it until it looks like your market. Fast, quotable, and worthless on its own, because the shrinking is where you smuggle in whatever answer you wanted. Bottom up starts from one customer. How many of them exist, what each one pays you in a year, multiplied. Slower, harder to fake, and the number an investor trusts. Value based asks what the problem currently costs the people who have it, and how much of that you could ever capture. It is the only one of the three that tells you whether the problem is worth solving at all.

Do all three and the answers will not match. Good. The gap is the information. A bottom-up number ten times below the top-down one usually means the industry report counted people who will never buy from you. A value-based number below your bottom-up one means you are planning to charge customers more than the problem costs them, which is a thing you should discover now rather than in a sales call.

Then the second slide. The four numbers from Module 01 become the unit economics slide, with the assumption written under each one in the smallest type on the page. Investors read that small type first. It is where they find out whether you are a founder who knows their own business or a founder who was handed a spreadsheet.

The work
[ AI OK ] USE THE TOOLS

Size your market three ways with a model, then take it apart. A prompt that works:

"Act as a skeptical analyst. Size the market for [what you sell] sold to [your customer] in [your region], three ways. Top down from published industry figures. Bottom up from the number of those customers multiplied by annual spend. Value based from what the problem currently costs them each year. Put every assumption on its own line and end each line with a source or the word GUESS. Do not adjust the three answers toward each other. If the sources disagree, show both."

Now audit it. Open every source it named. Some will not exist, and the fabricated ones look exactly like the real ones, which is why you open them rather than skim them. Every line ending in GUESS either gets a real number from your own fieldwork or gets deleted. If the three totals came back close together, the model smoothed them for you, and you should ask it again for the workings and check the multiplication yourself.

Then build the two slides. Market size, showing all three numbers and which one you stand behind. Unit economics, showing the four numbers from Module 01 with an assumption line under each. Read the assumption lines out loud to somebody. The ones you rush past are the ones you do not believe.

Arithmetic over other people's published numbers is exactly the work a founder hands to a tool. Catching the invented source is not faster with a tool, and it is the part the room is testing.

Deep dive: the market size question is not about the market optional

An investor who asks how big the market is already has an opinion about it. They have seen the industry reports, probably the same ones you found. The question is aimed at something else.

They are checking whether you built the number from your own customers or borrowed it. A founder who has talked to forty people knows how many exist, roughly, and can say so without reaching for a slide. A founder who has not will quote a figure with a B on the end and go quiet when asked how they got there.

Which is why the bottom-up number is worth the extra hour. It is the only one of the three that is downstream of your own fieldwork, and it is the one that survives follow-up questions.
