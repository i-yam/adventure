What a customer costs, what a customer is worth

01

In the first three months of 2017, Blue Apron spent sixty million dollars on marketing. It finished the quarter with a hundred and fifty-seven thousand more customers than it started with.

Three hundred and eighty-six dollars, to get one person to cook a box of chicken.

In the filing it handed the regulator that same year, the company reported a customer acquisition cost of ninety-four dollars. Lawyers read that document line by line before it went in. Nobody lied.

A venture fund called Origin Ventures took the same filing apart in public and came out above two hundred and fifty. Their teardown of the Blue Apron numbers lays the whole story out in one place, the marketing spend, the cohorts, the churn, with the charts they built to get there. Read it end to end. What follows here is only the part you need in order to do the arithmetic yourself.

Acquisition cost is money spent to get customers divided by customers you got. Every fight about it happens in the numerator and the denominator. Does the numerator include the discount you gave the new customer, the salary of the person running the ads, the free month? Does the denominator count everyone who signed up, or everyone still there ninety days later? Move those two lines and you can produce almost any number you want, in either direction, without writing anything false.

Then the other side. Blue Apron kept about thirty-one cents of every dollar after the food, the box, the ice packs and the freight. The average customer spent two hundred and thirty-six dollars in that quarter, which left the company roughly seventy-four dollars. That seventy-four is the contribution: what is left of a sale after the costs that exist only because the sale happened. Rent is not in there. The engineer's salary is not in there. Contribution is what the customer hands you to pay for everything else.

Now divide. At seventy-four dollars a quarter, a customer who cost three hundred and eighty-six takes a little over five quarters to pay for themselves. Call it sixteen months. That is the payback period, and it is the number that decides whether you can survive your own growth, because between the day you pay and the day you are repaid, the money is gone.

Card-transaction data from outside the company showed that more than half of Blue Apron's customers were gone by month three.

The customer who needs sixteen months to pay for themselves leaves in three.

Lifetime value is contribution per period multiplied by the number of periods they stay. Two quarters at seventy-four dollars is roughly a hundred and fifty dollars of lifetime value, set against three hundred and eighty-six spent to find them. The company was buying dollars for two dollars fifty.

Run the same customer against the ninety-four dollar figure and the business pays back inside one quarter and returns three times what it cost. Same customers. Same food. Different arithmetic.

Seed investors carry two conventions in their heads. Lifetime value should be around three times acquisition cost, and payback should land inside twelve months. Neither is a law. Both get quoted at you in Class 08, so know where yours sit and know why.

The work
[ NO AI ] DO THIS ONE YOURSELF

Four numbers, on paper, about your own thing. What does one customer pay you, and how often. What does it cost you to serve them, counting only the costs that appear because they bought. What have you actually spent, or would have to spend, to find one of them, counting the discount and your own time at some honest rate. How long do they stay, and say plainly whether that number is measured or hoped.

Write the four numbers down with the assumption sitting next to each one, in words, in a full sentence. If a number rests on nothing, write that. A blank you have labelled is worth more in Class 08 than a figure you cannot defend.

A model will hand you a lifetime value in four seconds and you will believe it, because it will be formatted beautifully. Do the arithmetic by hand once and you will never believe one again.

Deep dive: lifetime value is a forecast in a fact's clothes optional

Acquisition cost is history. You spent the money, the receipts exist, the argument is only about which receipts to count. Lifetime value is a prediction about behaviour that has not happened yet, dressed as a figure with two decimal places.

Blue Apron's own filing, which is still sitting in public on the regulator's servers, blended three years of customers into one curve. The 2014 group behaved differently from the 2015 group, which behaved differently from the 2016 group, and averaging them produced a shape that described nobody. Split customers by the month they arrived and follow each group separately, and the averaging stops hiding the trend. If every new group leaves faster than the one before it, the blended average will keep looking fine for about a year.

Money arriving in three years is also worth less than money arriving now, which is why a five-year lifetime value quoted to two decimal places should make you suspicious of everything else on the slide.

Class 09 takes this apart properly, by channel and over time. For Class 08 you need the four numbers and the honesty about which of them you measured.
