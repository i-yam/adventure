Cheap today, expensive in March

The
debt

Two people on your team are doing the same job. Nobody has said so. It is easier this way, both of them are good, and the overlap only costs you an argument every couple of weeks.

Horowitz calls this management debt. It works like technical debt. You take a decision that is cheap today because it avoids a hard conversation, and you pay it back later with interest, usually to somebody who did not take out the loan.

He names three that show up in almost every young company. Two people in one box, which feels like abundance until the day one of them has to be told they are not the owner. No performance feedback at all, which feels kind until someone is fired for something they were never told about. And the raise given because a good engineer got an offer somewhere else, which fixes one person for six months and teaches everyone else exactly how the pay system works.

The interest on all three is paid in trust, which does not appear in any model you have built in this course.

The work
[ NO AI ] DO THIS ONE YOURSELF

List three expedient decisions live on your team right now. Include the one you would rather not write down. That one is usually the expensive one, which is why your hand slowed.

Price each of them. Six months out, what does it cost, in a number, a date, or the name of the person who leaves. Vague costs get ignored, which is how they got to be six months old.

Take the most expensive one and write the first sentence of the conversation that clears it. The sentence itself, in the words you will use, out loud. Then put a date on it inside this week and tell your partner the date.

Last, the decision you have been avoiding because both sides have a real case. Write each case in three sentences. Then decide. Courage here is worth more than being right, because the two arguments were close enough that the delay was costing more than the wrong choice would.

The whole exercise is admitting something in your own handwriting. Outsourcing that produces a tidy document about a company nobody works at.

Deep dive: why the counteroffer is the worst of the three optional

The counteroffer looks like the cheapest of the three. One person, one number, problem solved by Friday.

What it actually does is publish a pricing rule. The rule is that the way to get paid properly here is to interview elsewhere and come back with paper. Everyone learns it, because the person who got the raise tells one friend and one friend is enough.

From then on your compensation is set by whichever competitor is recruiting hardest, and your most loyal people are the worst paid, which is a mechanism design problem of the kind Class 11 takes apart. You built a system where the rational move is disloyalty, then you get to be disappointed when people make it.
