1Asking a town that does not exist

Würzburg is arguing about a multifunctional arena: about 7,000 seats east of the main station, around a hundred events a year, concerts, congresses, the Würzburg Baskets and the Wolves. The estimated cost has gone from about €51 million in 2021 to about €85 million. The city contributes the land and a cash investment on top. A city-wide debate is planned for 10 December, after which the council decides whether to put the question to a vote.

Market research used to mean collecting people in a room. The lecturer ran it another way. NVIDIA publishes Nemotron-Personas-Germany, an open dataset of synthetic Germans built with Soofi, a consortium of German AI institutes and startups. None of these people exist. Each has a generated biography (profession, hobbies, travel and food tastes, career goals) plus the usual demographics: age, household, migration background, housing, education, employment, Kreis and Land. The generation is controlled so the overall distributions match official statistics. Filtering to Würzburg city and Landkreis gave about 3,600 personas.

Each persona went to a fresh model instance with the instruction: you are this person, what do you think of the arena? Answers came on a five-point scale. Ones and twos counted as against, fours and fives as for, threes as undecided. With no briefing the split was roughly 30% against, 40% for, 30% undecided.

Then a second, independent run. The same personas, no memory of their first answer, but now with a short neutral briefing: the cost history, the city's share, the timeline to 2029, the arguments for (a modern venue, bigger events, sport, hotels and gastronomy) and against (a strained budget, money that could go to schools and social spending, a venue too big for the town, parking). Information moved people towards no. A part of the supporters dropped off, and a large share of the undecided became opponents.

The simulated town also split along lines you could check against a real poll. The young were for it and support fell with age. People with children were for it, which is funny given how often opponents argue from children's needs. Employed people were for it and retirees strongly against. Renters were warmer than owners, and the Landkreis was more undecided than the city. Education and migration background barely mattered.

The whole run used about 13 million tokens and cost about €130. A 100-persona pilot fitted inside an ordinary chat subscription. Read more if you are interested.

2What synthetic people are good for

Nobody knows yet how well a model plays a particular human. That is an open research question. The dataset is calibrated nationally, so a single town is a rougher fit. Models are averaging machines. Expect too many people in the middle and too few at the extremes, so the variance comes out lower than in a real population.

Early on, though, you mostly care where the middle sits, and the middle is what models do best. If 67 of 100 synthetic users prefer interface A to interface B, real users in that segment probably lean the same way. That covers a lot of early decisions:

  • which of two designs to launch, without building an A/B test;
  • which feature belongs early on the user path;
  • pricing: what people say they would pay, and whether they prefer pay-as-you-go, a subscription, or instalments;
  • how any answer differs across segments, because the answer is stored as one more column next to the demographics.

Polls also miss turnout. An election is less a contest of ideas than a contest of mobilisation, and a simulation of opinions says nothing about who shows up to vote.

For comparison: at Yandex, more than ten years ago, a qualitative study meant paying an agency to find ten people from the segment and spending four hours with them in a stuffy room, at several hundred to several thousand per person. Synthetic panels are worse than that and much cheaper. The lecturer's bet is that within three to five years, running an idea past agents before running it past humans will be standard practice. The dataset sits on Hugging Face, and its Data Studio lets you take a sub-sample with SQL, for example families with children of a given age.

3A playground for maths

Only one team brought the eight-frame onboarding from last week. Their idea was a maths playground. A student watches a 3Blue1Brown-style video and lands on a site where the function from the video sits under sliders. Ask a random person what happens to ax³ + bx² + cx + d when a grows and they will not know. Here they move a and see it. An AI assistant can build further visualisations on request, and the syllabus extends to complex numbers, linear algebra and calculus.

The feedback came in two layers. First, the frames told a story but did not show screens. Where is the assistant? Where are the sliders relative to the video? Does a person know what to click? At a finer level of detail an onboarding often stops working.

Second, the frame is too small for the problem. In October 2026 OpenAI announced an AI proof of the quasi-Riemann hypothesis, a weaker cousin of the Riemann hypothesis, which is one of the Millennium Prize problems. Proofs like this are long dependency graphs of lemmas that no human reads line by line, and mathematicians are now arguing about what their job is. One position in that debate, associated with Terence Tao, is that more of the future work will be choosing which questions to point AI at. That needs people who can understand mathematics quickly and at scale, which means new ways to visualise it.

So the ambitious version is not a slider page for each topic, rebuilt by hand every time. It is a general method, perhaps a fine-tuned open model, that visualises any mathematical statement, from school algebra to research proofs. Specialisation of this kind works: Zhipu's GLM models are known for strong front-end design because their makers put deliberate effort into front-end data. The quick slider version is still worth shipping to see whether students like it, but it should not be the end goal.

One neighbour in the space is Aimathic from Munich, a voice tutor for school maths sold to schools. Students who struggle with maths struggle most at home, where nobody can help and typing formulas is a barrier, so voice helps. The tutor also runs on top of the school's own verified materials, chosen by the teacher. They are piloting in schools in Germany, Ukraine and the US, and the lecturer offered an introduction.

4Minimal and viable

The room's first answer to "MVP" was "most valuable player". Google agreed, which says more about who searches than about the term. Here it means the minimum viable product, sometimes prototype or proposition. The two words pull against each other.

Viable is decided by the customer. A viable product creates enough subjective value that someone pays for it. Minimal is decided by you: the least effort that gets there. Good founders try to land as close to viability as they can with as little effort as they can. That is not laziness. There is always more to do.

The trade-off has changed. An early founder can afford neither much market research nor much development. Both costs have dropped sharply, the first through tools like the synthetic panel above, the second through AI-assisted development, and nobody yet knows where the new balance sits.

5The man in the airport bar

The story is from Ben Horowitz's The Hard Thing About Hard Things. At Opsware, the company that grew out of Loudcloud, a single large customer was a huge share of the business. Then word came that it was leaving. A new decision-maker wanted the competitor's product.

The sales team researched him and found no bad blood and no rational reason to switch. Wining and dining did not help. Then someone noticed that whenever he travelled he asked for the longest possible connection, so he could sit in the airport bar. This was not a typical buyer, and they stopped treating him like one. What he wanted turned out to be an asset-management product that a competitor offered as part of a bundle, which he used for another part of his job. The maker of that product was Tangram, a small company in Cary, North Carolina. It cost less than one year of the customer's contract, so Opsware bought it, took it off the market so the competitor could no longer bundle it, and bundled it themselves. The customer stayed.

The point for an MVP is that business decisions get made for all sorts of reasons. A customer is entitled to want something you find illogical. While you polish the perfect product, someone else works out that the buyer is just weird, ships something worse that the buyer likes, and wins. Speed, relationships and likeability are part of go-to-market.

6Where to stop building

The line the lecturer uses with his own team: if you are not ashamed of your product, you over-engineered it. Reid Hoffman's version is about being embarrassed by your first release. You either get used to burning more effort than you need, every time, or you get used to being a little ashamed.

The practical test:

  1. Look at what you have built and list the one to three things you are most ashamed of. A model can help with the list.
  2. For the top item, ask whether fixing it would add value comparable to what you already ship.
  3. If yes, that is the next feature. If no, stop. That is your MVP.

Two examples from earlier projects in the course:

  • Warehouse planning. A layout tool that works for one hall configuration has a shameful gap: you do not know whether it works for any customer. Now find the most common type of storage building, say a rectangular hall without columns in a range of sizes, and brute-force test 40 configurations of it. The tool still does not work for everyone, but you are only ashamed in front of customers you do not need yet. The next item on the list, say humanoid robots in the layout, does not matter to the customers you can sell to. That is an MVP.
  • Automated social media for small firms at €1,000 a month. It works in English but not German and misses some networks. Tell the customer exactly what it does and does not do, and ask what that is worth. If they say €500 and pay it, you have an MVP. A missing feature is often just a pricing question.

7Hair on fire

This is why the course spent its first month on the pain point. If the pain is big enough, customers tell you what to build next. They write in to say they like it and need this and that, and the conversation becomes "how much, and by when?"

Marketing calls them customers with their hair on fire. Hand them an extinguisher and they do not read the label. If it puts the fire out they are yours, imperfections and all, and they may order a set for every salon they own.

Commitment also changes churn. Many more people read the menu at a restaurant's door and walk on than sit down, open it, and then leave. A customer who is using your product, even a bad one, stays much longer. In the lecturer's experience at Yandex and in startups, how quickly a founder responds matters more than how good the product is at the start. Users who reported problems and saw them fixed like you more than users who never had a problem. They feel they built it with you, and they bring the next customers.

How do you know you hit the pain point? When nothing happens there are two explanations: there is no pain, or nobody noticed you. Separate them. Hand the product to a small group who clearly have the problem and watch. If they do not use it as intended, or their current workaround turns out simpler than your solution, you missed. If they love it, your problem is go-to-market. Then experiment relentlessly: post from many angles hoping something goes viral, buy a little traffic and check retention, or try a PR stunt. One men's wellness app posted a deliberately outrageous job advert, got a flood of applications and worldwide coverage, and the downloads followed. There is no recipe for being noticed, only repeated trying.

8Choosing a co-founder

Paul Graham lists eighteen mistakes that kill startups. The lecturer's top three are the wrong co-founder, over-engineering and under-exploring the customer. Asked whether there is such a thing as a wrong co-founder: yes, the same way there are wrong partners and wrong friends.

A co-founder takes responsibility without being asked. They keep you informed but do not hand you their decisions. Some things are decided together, like bringing in someone new with equity or the terms of an investment. Inside their own area, they decide. The common mistake is picking a good employee and giving them co-founder equity. An employee asks, partly because asking moves the responsibility up. You can fire an employee for a bad call. Firing a co-founder is much harder, because they still own part of the company.

Honesty, which is close to fairness. In the lecturer's view relationships work until someone cheats and rarely after, and companies are the same. Nobody knows at the start how much each person will contribute. Illness, family, burnout and lost motivation all happen. The split has to feel fair to everyone, and when information stops flowing, one side starts feeling cheated while the other thinks all is well. At Pleias the lecturer's share was raised after the co-founders agreed he was doing more than planned. The reverse conversation also happens: the others sit you down, intervention-style, and ask you to recommit or give some equity back.

Can a contract do this? Partly. The usual tool is vesting: shares earned over time, four years being typical, often with a one-year cliff. A common shape is two founders splitting evenly while founding team members (a founding engineer, say) earn about 5% a year against agreed results. They carry less risk and may draw a salary early. The earlier the company, the harder it is to write commitments that still make sense a year later, and a stale commitment creates tension you do not need. Paperwork can cap your losses. It cannot build trust, and in some cultures insisting on documents even reads as distrust. Do not go into a venture with someone you do not trust, whatever they are willing to sign.

Co-founders carry the culture. An employee can suggest how the company should be. A founder decides. Something as small as whether people swear at work decides who will be happy there, and if you do not choose, the culture forms around whoever you hired. As the company grows, decisions get delegated, and culture that is not passed on deliberately fades. Anthropic started with seven co-founders on equal terms, an unusual choice that drew criticism and seems to work, and it interviews for values. Co-founders can be alike or complementary. Jobs and Wozniak were very different people. Page and Brin were very similar and brought in Eric Schmidt as the grounding third.

9Relax the assumption

Once you have the list of what blocks viability, the usual mistake is treating the problems as independent and attacking the hardest first. Brian Christian and Tom Griffiths, in Algorithms to Live By, use a wedding: 120 guests, fifteen tables, no table where two people hate each other. As stated it is very hard. Allow guests to be split between tables and it becomes easy. Seat everyone at random, split the people in conflict and move the halves to other tables, repeat until the hostility is low, then round back to whole people. Dropping one assumption is what made it solvable. Part 2 turns this into an exercise.

Founders find this hard, because a clear vision makes your assumptions feel like physics. Treating your customer and product as a set of changeable assumptions is one of the most productive habits you can have, in product and in negotiation.

An example from outside business: solutionism, the belief that every problem has a solution. Sometimes a person describes a problem because they want emotional support, not a fix, and offering a fix upsets them. The solver is then hurt not to be thanked. Drop the assumption that every stated problem asks for a solution and the conversation goes better for both of them.

10Hardware for kittens

After the break, teams said what they would pitch on 8 December. The first was hardware. Cats sleep on your face because they want warmth, as anyone who has fostered a hundred of them in animal protection knows. Lock them out and they scratch at the door. Wanda, named after a fostered mother cat, combines a microfibre donut bed, a heating element, a heartbeat and silicone teats into one warming and nurturing device for abandoned kittens.

The pet supply market is bigger than it looks until you touch it. Fifteen years ago someone in Russia built a business on cardboard cat scratchers and now has copycats. The idea also extends to dogs. Hardware brings three hard questions:

  • Unit economics. Margins are lower than in software, and you do not know your cost until you have produced something. The heartbeat and the teats add cost. Work out manufacturing, logistics and the price a pet owner will pay, perhaps as a birthday present for the pet.
  • China. You will almost certainly manufacture there. Production is organised in specialised clusters. One city makes most of the world's electric kettles, along with their heating elements, buttons and plastic housings, so supply chains are short and scale keeps margins thin. The catch is copycats: a small order with weak IP protection, and your device soon appears on Temu and AliExpress. The choice is between a controlled, expensive supply chain and an efficient one with the risk of copying, and successful products get copied in the end anyway. Speed of growth is the real defence.
  • Patents. A European or US patent protects the market where you sell, so copies can be stopped at that border. In China you need a Chinese patent and a Chinese lawyer. US courts can rule against an infringing Chinese company, but it can simply re-register. Whether a Chinese patent is worth it depends on whether China is a market for you.

A side point: regulation does not stop an activity. It changes its cost structure. When breaking a rule pays well enough, businesses price the risk in. The drug trade is the extreme case, and cartels oppose legalisation because legal pharmacies would undercut their margins. Effective regulation makes sure supply and demand never meet. It fails where demand barely responds to price, as with addiction.

11One step towards the pitch

The maths team asked what their MVP should be, since a general model for visualising mathematics cannot be built in two weeks. The answer used relaxation again. Drop the end goal for now and take one visible step towards it, so people can see the direction.

The "why now" is clear: LLMs have changed what mathematics is, and humans now need help understanding it. The missing piece, a model trained on how to explain hard concepts, is out of reach. In reach: pick a set of problems, see how current models explain them, and set your explanation next to theirs. Publish it, or show it to fifty students and report that half preferred yours to what Claude gives out of the box. That is not yet an MVP, because nobody pays for it, but it makes the problem concrete and shows how to scale. A viable version could be an intuitive "mathematics for AI" course on an existing course platform: a few free lessons, then pay for the rest. The pitch is still the big one: new AI that explains mathematics faster and more completely.

The team behind AI-run marketing for small businesses wants a dashboard showing each week's Google and Meta ad results and the orders behind them. Advice: find the segment where the need is sharpest and people will spend extra right now. Before Christmas that means present sellers. Dropshippers are a large market, since dropshipping is cheap products plus targeted marketing, but they are good at marketing themselves, so you would have to be better. People just starting a dropshipping business, who need a cheap way in, may be easier. The team's two pilot clients, an alternative health club and a removals company, are very different, so the product must handle that variety if the pitch is "small businesses in general".

Another team considered AI for fitness and motivation. Be warned: the lecturer heard two such pitches at a single accelerator in Yerevan on one weekend.

12Frustration

One team was visibly frustrated: little progress, little communication, and an immediate "it's everybody's fault". That reflex is worth noticing. Nobody had asked whose fault it was.

There is a name for this: creative frustration, what you feel when you work on a hard problem and cannot crack it. The cognitive literature suggests creative solutions rarely come first, so the feeling is healthy. It means you have used up the obvious options, or become critical enough to see they do not work. It is the valley that follows Dunning–Kruger optimism, where most projects die. If you came up with a pitch and felt no frustration at all, the bad news is the idea is probably not good.

Register it, and skip the blame. Frustration almost always comes with an urge to blame someone, yourself included, and blame wrecks a team. This is the difference between founder and employee from Section 8: the blame stops with the founder. Co-founders work out what went wrong and learn from it. They do not blame each other.

The lecturer once led a team of three for half a year on a product. A first pilot with a bank went well, the bank did not buy, there was no second customer within two months, and they stopped. He could have blamed the team. Instead he called it what it was: his wrong bet on a market that was not there. Taking responsibility and moving on is uncomfortable, and it is a luxury only a founder has. An employee who takes the blame risks the bonus or the job. A founder can simply drop the project and move on.

"Victory has a hundred fathers and defeat is an orphan." Good founders adopt the orphan. Jeff Bezos calls Amazon one of the best places in the world to fail. The Fire Phone flopped and led to a writedown of about $170 million, but Ian Freed, who led it, stayed and went on to work on the Echo and Alexa. Employees who know responsibility ends with you value you more the longer they stay.

13Thinking sideways

Creative work cannot be brute-forced. There is only so much useful time at a desk. Neurons that just fired tend to fire again in the same order, so new thoughts are physically harder than old ones. Thinking harder does not open new paths. It wears out the old ones. Frustration is that exhaustion.

Two moves work reliably:

  • Load the substrate. Stop generating solutions and learn about the problem from the side. What happens in this market, what the competitors do, where the customers go on holiday, what else bothers them. This rests the deciding part of the brain. When a lucky accident comes, from a conversation or something you see, it only helps if there is enough knowledge there for it to work on. Kekulé's dream of a snake biting its tail and the ring structure of benzene is the classic story.
  • Change the context. The verbal part of the brain is a thin layer, and much of the deciding happens below it. People fall in love irrationally and explain it well afterwards. Shift towards feelings: what you hate about the problem, what you love, why you want to build it. Walk, shower, listen to music. Walking raises visual stimulation, which is partly why lectures have slides. Studies suggest people learn more from educational videos while exercising, probably because the brain gets more oxygen.

Then the room tried it. Move one digit to make 100 = 102 true. Verbal attempts stalled. With eyes closed, imagine the six digits as planes that take off and land again in an order that works. Several people saw the 2 land out of line, below or apart from the others, before they could say why. The answer is to lift the 2 into an exponent: 100 = 10². The visual thinking found it before the verbal thinking could put it into words. When you work on your pitch, learn which kinds of input get you out of the loop, and trust them a little.

Two questions at the end. Should a team with an uncertain idea keep going? Yes. The course teaches you to fail well, not to succeed. Your real ventures are for succeeding. Is profit the goal? Eventually. Before product/market fit you often do not know your business model, so maximising profit means maximising scale. Profitability comes after fit.

14Summary and homework

  • Synthetic personas turn market research into an afternoon and about €130. Trust them for the middle of a distribution, not the tails, and not for turnout.
  • Viable is the customer's call: someone pays. Minimal is yours: the least effort that gets there.
  • Customers are not rational, and they are entitled to that. Sometimes winning means finding out what the odd buyer actually wants.
  • If you are not ashamed of your product, you over-engineered it. Fix what you are ashamed of only when fixing it adds as much value as you already ship.
  • Start with customers whose hair is on fire. Once they are using it, responding fast matters more than polish.
  • If nothing happens, separate "no pain" from "nobody noticed" before changing the product.
  • A co-founder takes responsibility, stays honest about contributions, and sets the culture. Contracts limit losses but do not create trust.
  • Stuck? Drop a constraint, solve the easier problem, then see how much of the constraint you need back.
  • Frustration is a sign of real work. Notice it, skip the blame, then feed your brain sideways instead of pushing harder.
