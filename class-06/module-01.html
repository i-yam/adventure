The
struggle

In 2003 Opsware kept losing deals to a competitor called BladeLogic. Same buyers, same demos, comparable price. They lost anyway.

Ben Horowitz went to his engineering leaders with three ideas, none of which involved building a better product. Sell the company. Partner with someone larger and ride their sales force. Reposition into a corner of the market where the losses would not show up in the numbers. Bill Turpin heard him out and told him there were no silver bullets for this, only lead bullets. The product was worse. Somebody had to go make it better.

They rewrote it. It took most of a year. Then they started winning, and a few years after that the company sold for one and a half billion dollars.

Horowitz has a name for the stretch between knowing you are losing and knowing what to do about it. He calls it the Struggle, and the honest content of the idea is that there is no content. No framework fires. The advice you paid for stops at the edge of your specific situation. You sleep badly, you consider selling, and what separates the founders who come out the far side is mostly that they were still there in the morning.

Everything in this course so far has been a tool for a problem with a shape. Interviews have a shape. A canvas has boxes. What arrives in month eight has no shape at all, and the first skill is telling the two kinds apart quickly, because you will waste weeks applying a method to something no method covers.

There is one version of this you will meet early enough to prepare for. Something is not selling. The comfortable explanation is that people have not understood it yet, which is a story problem, and better words fix it. The other explanation is that your thing is worse than what they are already using, and no words fix that. Founders reach for the comfortable one because it is cheaper by about nine months. The test is whether somebody who understood your offer completely still walked away. If they did, go build.

The work · 45 minutes
[ NO AI ] DO THIS ONE YOURSELF

Take the thing in your project you have been avoiding. Not the hardest thing, the one you keep finding good reasons to postpone. Write one page describing it with no solution anywhere in it. Facts only, including the ones that make you look bad.

Then answer one question in writing. Story problem or product problem? Go get the evidence rather than deciding at your desk. Find somebody who understood the offer completely and said no, and write down what they chose instead and why they chose it. If nobody has said no to you yet, you have not asked enough people, and that is your finding for the week.

Keep the page. Class 08 will ask which one it was and whether you called it right.

A model will hand you three options and a recommendation in four seconds. The work here is the fifteen minutes where you have nothing.

Reading
Paul Graham · The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups

Eighteen ways to die, and the first one is still making something nobody wants. Read it before Class 07.

Deep dive: when the better product loses anyway optional

Horowitz says go build a better product. His business partner spent a whole essay arguing that in a dead market the better product loses to a worse one and both of them starve, because the market is the only thing that matters. Both men are describing companies they actually ran.

The difference is which question you have already settled. Lead bullets assume the demand is real and somebody else is serving it better than you are. If nobody is buying from anyone, nine months of rewriting buys you an excellent product in an empty room. Before you commit, check that your competitor is taking real money from real people. A rival with revenue is the cheapest proof of a market you will ever get, which is one reason competition is worth less panic than founders give it.
