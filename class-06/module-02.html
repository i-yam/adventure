Two
moves

Seat a hundred and twenty wedding guests at fifteen tables so that nobody sits with someone they cannot stand. Checking every possible arrangement would take longer than the marriage.

Christian and Griffiths take that problem apart in Algorithms to Live By, and the answer has nothing to do with a faster computer. You cheat. Drop the rule that a guest has to occupy exactly one seat, allow people to sit in fractions, and the impossible version collapses into an easy one that solves in seconds. Then you round the fractions back into whole people. The answer comes back imperfect, close, and with a known bound on how far off it can be, which is more than you had a minute ago.

That move is called relaxation, and founders need it constantly without knowing the name. The perfect roadmap. The org chart that leaves nobody unhappy. The price that maximizes revenue across every segment at once. All of them are the wedding table. You pick a constraint you have been treating as physics, drop it on purpose, solve what is left, and then see how much of the constraint you actually need to put back.

The second move is for a different failure. You are not stuck at an impossible problem, you are stuck at a decent answer and cannot see a better one anywhere near it. Climbing uphill gets you to the top of whichever hill you happened to be standing on. The fix in the algorithms is to jump at random, violently at first and more gently as you go, so the process falls off the small hill and finds the bigger one behind it.

In practice that means sampling instead of surveying, and doing one thing on purpose that is not the obvious next thing. Call a customer picked at random instead of your favorite one. Ship to the segment you wrote off in week two. Quote the price you consider ridiculous on exactly one deal and watch the face.

The work · 15 minutes
[ NO AI ] DO THIS ONE YOURSELF

Take the page from Part 1. Write out every constraint sitting on that problem, including the ones about money, timing, and what other people will agree to. Then cross out the one that feels least removable. Solve that version in ten minutes and write the answer down even if it is useless.

Then one random move. Pick something by coin flip rather than judgment, a customer to call, a feature to cut, a number to quote, and commit to doing it before the next class.

A model optimizes inside the constraints you hand it, and it will never tell you that one of them was imaginary. Deciding which rule to break is the part you cannot pass along.

Deep dive: how you know the cheat is safe optional

The relaxed answer is always at least as good as the real one, because you solved an easier problem than the one you have. That makes it a ceiling, and a ceiling is useful information on its own.

If dropping your hardest constraint improves the outcome by four percent, that constraint was never what was hurting you and the argument about it can stop. If it improves the outcome by two hundred percent, you have found the thing worth spending a quarter on. The gap between the relaxed answer and your best real answer is a measurement of how much room is left to fight for, which beats arguing about it in a meeting.
