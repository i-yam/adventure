The
bolts

Uber's core platform contribution margin in 2018 was nine percent.

Nine cents on the dollar, after the cost of actually running the trip. It is not the gross margin a software founder quotes on a slide. It is what survives once the variable cost of delivering the thing has been paid. Every LTV in this room sits on a number like that, and most of them borrowed gross margin instead, because gross margin is prettier and nobody checks.

The naive LTV is revenue per customer times margin, divided by churn. It has four soft spots.

Average churn hides the cohort. Customers do not leak at a constant rate. They leave fast in the first weeks and the survivors stay for years, which is the retention curve you drew in Class 05. Use the curve you measured, not the average that flattens it.

Gross margin flatters. Run the whole thing on contribution margin, after support, payments, hosting, and whatever else a live customer costs you every month.

Money later is worth less than money now, so discount it. Twenty percent a year is not unreasonable for a company that may not exist in three.

And then the horizon. An LTV that runs to infinity is a way of spending a decade of revenue nobody has earned yet. Cap it at thirty six months and be ready to defend the cap.

Only then the ratio. Three to one is the number people repeat in bars. On its own it means nothing, because three to one with a nine month payback and three to one with a thirty month payback are different companies, and only one of them lives through a bad quarter.

Deep dive: where contribution margin and gross margin stop agreeing optional

Gross margin takes revenue and removes the cost of goods sold. Contribution margin removes everything that varies with one more customer, which usually includes support tickets, payment fees, the free tier they sit on for two months first, and in a marketplace the money you pay the supply side to show up. At ten customers the two numbers are close enough to ignore. At ten thousand the gap is the whole argument.

Uber is the clean illustration. Take rate looks like a software margin. Then driver incentives, promotions, insurance and support get subtracted and nine percent is what is left of the core platform. The slide said marketplace. The accounts said something with a lot of variable cost in it.
