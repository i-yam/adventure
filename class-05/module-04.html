Write the kill rule first

A fund does not write you one cheque. It writes the smallest cheque that gets you to the next fact.

Seed money buys the answer to one question, and that answer decides whether there is a Series A. The useful part is the timing. The question and the number are agreed while nobody knows how it turns out. Later, when the founder is in love with the thing and the investor has money in it, neither of them would agree to that number again.

You have the same problem at student scale. Three weeks from now you will have data about your prototype. You will also, by then, have spent three weeks on it, plus a weekend you did not plan on. The version of you who has spent three weeks is not the person you want interpreting the numbers.

So write the rule now, while it is cheap and you are still cold about it.

The work · 15 minutes
[ NO AI ] DO THIS ONE YOURSELF

Three sentences on one page, dated. What result by Class 08 means you keep going. What result means you stop and go find a different problem. The day you look at it. Sign the page and give a copy to someone else in the room, someone who will hold it up on the day and read it back to you.

Stopping is a legitimate outcome here. A student who kills a dead idea in week eight and turns up in Class 12 with evidence for a live one will pitch better than a student who spent four weeks defending a corpse.

A model will happily help you write a kill rule with a door in it, because it is agreeable and you are the one asking. Build your own trap.

Deep dive: why late pivots cost so much more optional

Explore and exploit is a trade against a clock. Trying a new thing is worth more when you have a long horizon left to enjoy the payoff, and worth almost nothing on your last day. This is why an eight-week-old company should try five markets and a five-year-old company usually should not.

Your term is a short horizon. The pivot that costs you a week in Class 05 costs you the whole pitch in Class 11.

In Class 02 you set a rule for when to stop looking. This is the other end of the same instrument, the rule for when to stop holding on.
