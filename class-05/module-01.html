Nobody buys a technology

01

In 2010 the phone in your pocket already knew where it was. Card payments online were a decade old. Black cars sat parked between airport runs with the meters off and the drivers bored.

Every part of Uber was lying around in plain sight. Nobody was buying any of it.

What people paid for was the end of standing on a corner with an arm in the air, not knowing whether a car was coming, whether the driver would take a card, or what it would cost when you got there. UberCab put a button on that. The button was ordinary. What it removed was not.

You will be tempted to describe your startup by what it is made of. Founders do this constantly. They say computer vision, they say marketplace, they say platform, and the room nods and nothing has been said. Customers do not buy the parts. They buy the thing that stops happening to them.

Alexander Osterwalder built the Business Model Canvas so a whole business fits on one wall instead of in a forty-page plan. Nine boxes. You will fill all nine of them before this block is over. Today you fill one.

The canvas. Everything else in Block 2 hangs off the middle box.

The value proposition sits in the centre because the other eight boxes are downstream of it. Change who you serve and your channels change. Change the pain and your pricing changes. Start anywhere else and you will spend the term decorating a guess.

The work · 45 minutes
[ NO AI ] DO THIS ONE YOURSELF

Write your value proposition in three sentences. The first names one customer segment narrow enough that you could walk to three of them this week. The second names the pain in the words you actually heard in your Block 1 interviews, not in yours. The third names what those people do about it today and what makes your version worth the cost of switching away from that.

Then read the three sentences aloud to someone from another team and say nothing else. If their first question is what the product actually is, your draft is a description of your technology and you write it again.

Your interview notes are the only evidence you own. A model that has never met your customer will fill this box with sentences that sound right, and you will believe them because they sound right.

Deep dive: two houses, one argument optional

Paul Graham tells founders to launch, get users any way they can, and let the users tell them what the thing is. Marc Andreessen wrote that the market is the only thing that matters, that a great team in a dead market loses every time, and that in a real market the product only has to be adequate.

Graham hands you a shovel. Andreessen hands you a map and says the shovel is beside the point.

Both of them are describing something they watched happen many times. Graham is watching founders who have not started. Andreessen is watching founders who have been grinding on a good product for three years in a market of four hundred people. You are the first kind right now. You will be the second kind faster than you expect.
