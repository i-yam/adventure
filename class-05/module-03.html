Sean Ellis ran growth at Dropbox and LogMeIn and got tired of arguing about whether a product was working.

So he asked users one question. How would you feel if you could no longer use this product. Very disappointed, somewhat disappointed, not disappointed. He asked it across a lot of companies and put the line at forty percent. Above it, growth was mostly a matter of effort. Below it, effort went into a bucket with a hole in the bottom.

The second signal costs nothing and lies less. Take everyone who first used your thing in a given week, count how many are still using it a month later, then two months, then three. Plot it. Every curve drops at the start. The only question is whether it keeps falling to zero or whether it bends and goes flat. A flat tail, even a low one, means some group of people found something they cannot get anywhere else. That group is the company.

Absence is harder to see, because absence looks like enthusiasm. People tell you the idea is great and never open it again. The pilot keeps getting rescheduled by mutual agreement. Nobody has told a friend unless you asked them to. The sale takes four calls and the buyer never once argues about the price, because they were never going to buy.

The work · 15 minutes
[ AI OK ] USE THE TOOLS

Pick one number you will report in Class 08. Write down what you expect it to be, and the value below which you would say out loud that this is not working. Two numbers, one sentence, committed before you have any data.

Then hand your metric to a model and tell it to attack.

Here is my product in one sentence, and the single metric I plan to report at an investor pitch in three weeks. Argue that the metric is gameable. Name three ways I could hit the number without anyone actually wanting the product. Then name the failure this metric would not detect at all.

Read what comes back, keep the attacks, and check every benchmark it gives you. It will produce industry averages to two decimal places with no idea where they came from, and it will hand you forty percent as though it were a law of physics rather than one man's rule of thumb drawn from a set of companies that were not yours.

Attacking your own number is the one part of this a model does better than you, because it has no stake in the answer.

Deep dive: the number starts lying the day you name it optional

Charles Goodhart was writing about monetary policy when he pointed out that a measure stops being a good measure once it becomes a target. It is the most reliable law in management and everyone breaks it anyway.

You have already seen the shape of this in Class 02, when you overfit a strategy to three loud interviews. Same failure, different costume. A team told to raise weekly active users will raise weekly active users, and one of the cheapest ways to do that is to send more email.

You get this back in Class 09, when the metric has money attached to it and a marketing budget behind it.
